About

My full name is Hoàng Đào Mỹ Duyên, and I go by Jenny Hoang. I study law and business administration at Ho Chi Minh City University of Law, in a programme taught in English.

The short version

I spent most of high school in competitive debate, first as a debater and then running my school's club. At university I worked in a legal and HR role alongside my studies and, from my second year, joined student research projects. Almost all of that research used the same method: take a concrete problem in Vietnamese law, look at how a few other countries deal with it, and work out what is worth borrowing and under which conditions.

Over time the topics moved toward technology: from proof in administrative cases, to ride-hailing drivers' contracts, to collecting electronic data, to personal data in criminal evidence. In 2026 I coordinated two larger course projects, one of which needed a small piece of software.

What changed in 2026

The two 2026 course projects were bigger than anything I had done before. In the CISG project, the numbers we collected did not support the conclusion our topic seemed to invite, and we had to choose between stretching the evidence and narrowing the claim. We narrowed the claim. In the same project I specified and tested a small research tool with AI help, which meant debugging Python and learning to use GitHub.

In the juvenile justice project, finding the rules was the easy part. Keeping their legal force straight was harder: a treaty, a committee's interpretation, a non-binding UN standard and a foreign statute each do different work in an argument.

What I try to hold to

  • Work out the conditions before the conclusion.
  • Say how strong a source is, as well as what it says.
  • When the evidence is thin, make the claim smaller.
  • Be clear about who did what, including where AI tools helped.

The clearest example is the turning point in the CISG project. The approach page goes through the method step by step.

What I am still learning

I have not yet worked with United States or EU privacy law in a structured way, done compliance work on a software product, or run interviews or fieldwork. I can specify, test and debug code with AI help, but I would not call myself a software engineer. I have not settled on a long-term specialisation; technology and data law is what I am trying now.
