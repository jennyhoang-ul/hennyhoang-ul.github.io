About

I am a fourth-year student in a dual Law and Business Administration degree, taught in English, at Ho Chi Minh City University of Law. Most of what I have learned about research I learned by doing it with a team and having to defend the result.

How I got here

Before university I spent four years in competitive debate, first as a debater and then as president of my school's club, where I wrote a training programme for 40 members and coached 21 newcomers. Debate is where I first practised building an argument for a side I had not chosen and answering the strongest version of the other side.

At university I began student research in my second year. The four projects I have worked on since 2023 follow the same pattern: take a concrete problem in Vietnamese law, study how selected other countries handle it, and ask what is worth borrowing and under what conditions. Over time the topics moved toward technology: from proof in administrative cases, to ride-hailing drivers' contracts, to collecting electronic data messages, to protecting personal data when authorities gather electronic evidence.

Alongside my studies I worked for about a year and a half as a legal and HR officer at an education company, drafting labour contracts and internal policies. In that role I wrote legal text for readers who were not lawyers.

Documented Awards and defence results are listed with decision numbers on the projects page.

What 2026 changed

Two course projects in 2026 were larger than my earlier work. In the CISG project, the numbers we collected did not support the conclusion our topic seemed to invite, and I had to decide whether to soften the evidence or narrow the claim. We narrowed the claim. In the same project I specified and tested a small research tool with AI coding assistance, which meant debugging Python and working through GitHub.

In the juvenile justice project, the hard part was not finding rules but keeping their legal force straight: a treaty, a committee's interpretation, a non-binding UN standard and a foreign state's statute each do different work in an argument.

What I hold to in research

  • State the conditions before the conclusion.
  • Say how strong a source is, not only what it says.
  • Prefer a narrower claim that holds over a broader one that does not.
  • Be clear about who did what, including when AI tools helped.

These are rules I can point to in my own work, not slogans; the case studies show where each one changed a decision.

What I still need to learn

I have not yet worked with United States or EU privacy law in a structured way, done compliance work on a software product, or carried out interviews or fieldwork. My coding is AI-assisted: I can specify, test and debug, but I would not describe myself as a software engineer. I have not fixed a long-term specialisation; technology and data law is the direction I am now testing.

Beyond research

I founded two small student initiatives: AMPLE, which ran a two-day debate tournament for about 150 participants with VND 35 million in sponsorship, and The Shift, a website with a series of law-focused content for students.
