Approach

The same sequence shows up in most of my projects. Each step below has a real example, so you can check it against the work.

Eight steps, each with an example

  1. Narrow the question

    The juvenile justice topic came down to one group (sentenced prisoners aged 14 to under 18) and one right (contact with family).

  2. Find out whether the rule applies

    Before asking what courts did with the CISG, we checked, step by step, whether it should have applied at all.

  3. Rank the sources

    A treaty, a committee's interpretation, a non-binding standard and a foreign statute carry different weight. I label which is which.

  4. Compare on fixed questions

    Seven questions asked of every source in the juvenile justice project, and the same comparative frame in all four student research projects.

  5. Check the claim against the evidence

    25 of 27 judgments not mentioning the CISG measured recognition, not interpretation. We changed the claim.

  6. Write down competing readings

    Two readings of the misconduct rule for young prisoners, each with its own fix.

  7. Make it usable

    A screening tool for the CISG team; a list of reforms ordered by who can make them and what they cost.

  8. Mark what is uncertain

    The tags on this site do that job: documented, self-reported, in progress, not yet confirmed.

I have used this on Vietnamese, international and some foreign law. I have not yet used it on United States or EU technology law, which is where I want to take it next.
