When the law applies but the judgment does not mention it

A course project on how Vietnamese courts identify and apply the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG), and what our evidence could and could not say about "homeward" interpretation.

Setting
Group 3, course on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (class CLCQTL47B), Ho Chi Minh City University of Law

The research problem

A dispute with a foreign party does not automatically fall under the CISG. Article 1 turns on where the parties have their places of business, whether those states are Contracting States at the relevant time or conflict-of-laws rules lead to one, what is being sold and whether the parties excluded the Convention. And even when the CISG is a relevant source, a court may apply it, cite it, consider and reject it, or not mention it at all.

Our topic asked about "homeward trend": interpreting the Convention through domestic-law concepts instead of autonomously, as Article 7 requires. That question only makes sense once a court has recognised the CISG in the first place.

Research questions

  1. How should a Vietnamese "foreign-element" dispute be distinguished from an international sale under CISG Article 1?
  2. How can each case be checked for CISG applicability with objective, repeatable steps?
  3. Where the CISG was applicable, did the court apply it, cite it, reject it or ignore it?
  4. Is the pattern evidence of homeward interpretation, or of something earlier: not recognising the Convention?

My role

I organised the project into four linked parts for a 30-minute presentation: theory and data; case analysis and the homeward test; mechanisms and a conditional forecast; and recommendations. I asked for a screening method grounded in each legal condition, set requirements for the team's statistics work (case versus document level, correct denominators, missing data, a codebook), and directed the revision of part 3 when the data did not support our original framing.

The dataset, slides and analysis are team outputs. I do not claim to have reviewed every document myself.

Method: a screening sequence

Rather than searching judgments for the word "CISG", we tested each document in order and recorded the reason at every step:

  1. Foreign element

    Does Vietnamese law treat the dispute as having a foreign element? A first filter only.

  2. Places of business

    Are the relevant places of business in different states (Articles 1 and 10)?

  3. Route and time

    Direct application under Article 1(1)(a), or via conflict-of-laws rules under 1(1)(b); Contracting State status on the relevant date (Article 100).

  4. Scope

    Excluded goods (Article 2) and mixed or manufacturing contracts (Article 3).

  5. Exclusion

    Did the parties opt out (Article 6)? Choosing Vietnamese law is not automatically an opt-out.

  6. Issue and court treatment

    Is the disputed issue governed by the Convention? Only then: how did the court actually treat it?

The key design choice was to keep our conclusion about applicability separate from what the court wrote.

Of the 27 relevant documents, 25 did not mention the Convention. 18 fell under the direct route of Article 1(1)(a), and 24 were pure sales contracts. In the 16 documents that were both, 14 still did not mention the CISG, so the gap cannot be explained only by difficult conflict-of-laws questions or hard-to-classify contracts.

Legal reasoning in practice

Domestic law in the reasoning is not automatically wrong

In a Binh Duong judgment the court applied the CISG to payment and interest but used Vietnamese law to set the interest rate. Article 78 gives a right to interest without fixing a rate, so turning to another source is not itself an error. The real question is whether the court followed Article 7(2): general principles of the Convention first, then the applicable national law. We treated this as an open question, not as proof of a homeward trend.

Procedural context changes what a citation proves

A Hai Phong decision cited the CISG while deciding whether to recognise a foreign arbitral award. A court reviewing an award is not deciding the contract on the merits, so that citation shows awareness of the Convention but says little about how merits courts interpret it.

The turning point

It would have been easy to divide 25 by 27 and call it a measure of homeward interpretation. But "the court did not mention the CISG" and "the court interpreted the CISG through domestic concepts" are different events. Two cases of actual application cannot show a stable interpretive trend.

So we changed the claim. The finding became a gap between applicability and recognition. Part 3 turned into a conditional forecast along two variables: how often courts recognise the CISG, and how well they interpret it autonomously. The highest-risk scenario is not courts ignoring the Convention, but recognition rising faster than the ability to interpret it correctly.

From research to a research tool

To keep seven people screening the same way, I specified a small web application and built it iteratively with AI coding assistance, in Python and Streamlit, through GitHub. The version I inspected has four tabs:

  • Screening: upload PDFs (exact duplicates are caught by file hash), walk through the legal steps, and save each answer with its reasoning.
  • Records: review, edit and manage the shared case records.
  • Statistics: aggregate the coded records.
  • Practice: re-screen a finished case and be stopped at the first step that differs from the team's answer; practice answers never touch the research data.

One rule I insisted on: the conflict-of-laws helper suggests an applicable law only when the facts it needs are recorded. If they are missing, it stops and asks for human review, because a missing fact must not silently become a "no".

My part was the legal logic, the requirements, integrating and editing code, and testing and reporting real errors (syntax, indentation and type errors) until they were fixed. Much of the code itself was written with AI assistance.

What it demonstrates, and what I developed

Demonstrated: applicability analysis of a treaty, case analysis that looks at reasoning rather than outcomes, disciplined use of data, and coordinating a team's research.

Developed during the project: turning legal rules into a decision sequence someone else can follow, reading error messages and fixing code with AI help, and presenting a narrow finding clearly.

Not shown by this project: expertise in privacy law, product compliance or software engineering.

Limitations and further questions

  • The sample is the team's collection, not a representative national dataset.
  • Our legal coding has not been audited by anyone outside the team.

The question I would pursue next is whether arbitral tribunals in Vietnam handle the CISG differently from courts; I am drafting an outline for that in my research notes.
