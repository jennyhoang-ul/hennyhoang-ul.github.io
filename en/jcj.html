A higher ceiling is not a guaranteed floor

A course project asking whether children serving prison sentences in Vietnam can rely on regular, accessible and protected contact with their families, measured against UN standards.

The research problem

Vietnam's 2024 Law on Juvenile Justice raised the limits on family visits and calls for prisoners aged 14 to under 18. But a larger allowance does not tell you how much contact a child can actually count on. That depends on who counts as family, what the calls cost, who listens, what happens after a disciplinary breach, and whether a complaint can be resolved before the missed visit stops mattering.

Research questions

  1. What entitlements, conditions and safeguards govern family contact under the 2024 juvenile justice law and the 2025 law on execution of criminal judgments, and how do the two interact?
  2. How does this compare with UN obligations and standards on relationships, regularity, access, privacy, restrictions and remedies?
  3. Which gaps justify legislative or administrative responses?

My role

I worked on the introduction and first chapter with another team member: defining the population (sentenced prisoners aged 14 to under 18, not pre-trial detainees or reformatory pupils), the research questions and the legal framework. Across the project I pushed for primary sources, exact citations, consistent terms, and claims no stronger than their sources. I reviewed the comparative chapter and the recommendations, which other members were assigned to draft, and prepared an English question-and-answer script for part of the presentation.

Vietnamese legal framework

2019 law (Article 76)2024 law (Article 166)
In-person visitsAt most 3 a month, up to 3 hours eachAt most 4 a month, up to 4 hours each; may extend to 24 hours under conditions
Phone or video contact in VietnamAt most 4 calls a month, up to 10 minutesAt most 4 a month, up to 15 minutes; urgent-case exception
Guaranteed minimumNone statedNone stated

The figures improved, but every one of them is a maximum. The project's central observation is that a higher ceiling does not create an enforceable floor. The 2025 general law and its circulars still govern letters, visitor approval, supervision and complaints, so the two laws have to be read together.

Berlin, England and Wales, Queensland, CaliforniaForeign state or regional law and standardsDrafting techniques: a statutory visiting floor, monitoring rules, contact as a basic entitlement, free calls

The four foreign examples were chosen purposively because each addresses one Vietnamese gap. They are not a representative sample and not evidence that those systems work better in practice.

Comparative method: seven criteria

Instead of describing each system in turn, the paper asked the same seven questions of every source and sorted each gap into a type.

CriterionQuestion asked
1. RelationshipsWho may the child stay in contact with?
2. Frequency and durationHow often and for how long, and is any of it guaranteed?
3. Visiting conditionsIs a visit private and practically reachable?
4. Channels and digital safeguardsHow are letters, calls and video regulated, paid for and monitored?
5. Special family eventsWhat happens in illness, bereavement or when family live abroad?
6. Restrictions and disciplineCan contact be cut as punishment?
7. Complaints and remediesCan the child get a timely, confidential remedy?

Each gap was labelled as an internal ambiguity between two Vietnamese texts, a normative shortfall against a non-binding standard, or an implementation risk that would need data to confirm. None was presented as a proven violation.

Applied reasoning: does the misconduct rule reach children?

The general 2025 law (Article 49(1)) reduces family visits after a prison-rule breach. The juvenile law sets its own contact regime (Article 166) and its own disciplinary rules (Article 169(1)). Which governs?

Does the general visit reduction apply to prisoners under 18?
Reading A: the juvenile law displaces itAn official interpretation or circular could make this clear.
Reading B: the general rule still appliesA circular cannot override a statute, so the law itself would need amending.

We set out both readings instead of asserting one, and proposed protecting baseline contact under either. This was an interpretive problem drawn from the texts, not a client matter.

Monitoring phone and video contact

The law permits supervision of calls. Drawing on the CRC's privacy article and General Comment No. 25, the paper separated watching a call from listening to it, recording it, keeping the recording and giving others access, and proposed a two-tier model: routine supervision by default, content review only for a documented risk and with procedural controls.

Why this matters beyond prisons. The same distinctions (observe, record, retain, access) are the ones privacy law asks of any service that handles a child's communications. The method transfers; the legal rules do not.

Key findings

The group assessed the framework as partly compatible with the UN material. It improves allowances and recognises audiovisual contact. But maxima without minima, domestic-only remote contact, cost, broad monitoring, uncertain status for non-family carers, thin procedures for family emergencies and slow complaints can each limit real protection.

Recommendations

The draft proposes eight reforms, staged by who can make them and what they cost:

  1. A guaranteed minimum under the existing monthly limits.
  2. No disciplinary cuts to baseline contact.
  3. A clear category for people significant to the child who are not relatives.
  4. Funded contact, including safeguarded calls with family abroad.
  5. Two-tier, proportionate monitoring.
  6. Prompt notice of serious family events and a reviewable compassionate-leave decision.
  7. Fast, confidential complaints with interim relief.
  8. Explicit rules for digital contact services.

These are proposals in a student draft, not adopted reforms.

Limitations and reflection

This was desk research: no interviews, prison visits or data on how often contact actually happens. Official 2023 statistics predate the 2026 rules and cannot measure their effect. Foreign provisions need a final version-by-version check.

The method the project relied on: a right written in a statute has to be followed down to procedure, cost, discretion and remedy before you can say whether it protects anyone.
